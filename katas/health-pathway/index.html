Health · Pathway

From a growth reference table to a checked BMI-for-age percentile.

Four katas on CDC’s BMI-for-age growth charts, with six fictional children. Each step has a file you can open and a result that CI computes again on every pipeline. Practice only: nothing here is a clinical assessment or medical advice.

Start with HLT-001Download the scores

01 / The files

Two public tables, fictional children, every number rerun

CDC’s National Center for Health Statistics publishes the BMI-for-age growth charts as data files: for each sex and month of age from 2 to 20 years, the parameters that turn a child’s body mass index into a percentile. Its pages give the method, four worked examples and the categories. The script reproduces CDC’s printed values before it scores anyone. The six children are made up by the Dojo, so anyone can check every result and no real child’s measurements are used.

Not medical advice. The children are fictional and nothing on this page is a screening, a clinical assessment or a diagnosis. BMI is a screening measure, not a diagnosis; a real child needs a qualified clinician.

  • The 2000 table and the 2022 extended table: CDC’s files, unchanged (CSV).
  • The fictional children: sex, age, height and weight (CSV).
  • The scores (CSV) and the same with each table row used (JSON).
  • The table inspection and the reference checks (JSON).
  • The result summary (Markdown) and every result under the manifest, with the method and the input hashes.
  • The pathway script (Python 3.12, standard library only, MIT), so you can rerun every number yourself.
  • README: sources, terms, how to rerun the checks, and what they do not cover.

02 / The steps

Four katas, in order

Each kata has a brief, prompts, checks and the evidence for the checks the Dojo has run. They are drafts: the Dojo built the example and CI checks it, and no learner has run them yet. Do the foundations first: Compare AI instructions and Spot a context trap.

  1. HLT-001 Inspect a growth reference

    Read CDC’s two BMI-for-age tables: their columns, how sex and age select a row, the repeated header and the rows no age selects.

  2. HLT-002 Compute a BMI-for-age percentile

    Reproduce CDC’s worked examples, then score fictional children with the LMS method and, above the 95th percentile, the extended method.

  3. HLT-003 Report the categories with their limits

    Place each child in CDC’s child and teen BMI categories, severe obesity included, and say what the result is not.

  4. HLT-R01 Health Randori: an unseen change

    A partner reveals one changed choice after you start; predict how the percentile and category move, then recompute.

The tables (HLT-001)

Each file has 219 rows for boys and 219 for girls, ages 24 to 240.5 months. Age is listed at the half month: 48.5 months stands for 48.0 up to, not including, 49.0 months (CDC). So a child’s completed months plus 0.5 selects the row; the rows at exactly 24 and 240 months are never selected. The 2000 file repeats its header before the rows for girls, at line 221. L is negative in all 438 rows, which caps the LMS z-score: depending on the row, it can never pass a value between 2.785 and 11.861. The two files share L, M and S, as CDC states. Result (JSON).

04 / The tools and sources

No packages, public sources

The script uses only the Python 3.12 standard library (its statistics.NormalDist gives the normal distribution), so it runs offline with nothing to install. A spreadsheet works for checking one child by hand. The Armory lists the stack recipe Python standard library.

  • CDC, Extended BMI-for-age growth charts: data files, last reviewed by CDC on 2 September 2024: the method above and below the 95th percentile, and the four worked examples.
  • The data files: bmiagerev.csv (CDC 2000 growth charts) and bmi-age-2022.csv (2022 extended charts).
  • CDC, Child and teen BMI categories: the five categories for ages 2 through 19 and the worked example. CDC, Adult BMI categories: the cut-offs from age 20 that Randori card D misapplies.
  • CDC cites National Health Statistics Reports no. 63 (2013) for how the LMS parameters were built, and Annals of Human Biology 47(6):514-521 (2020) for the method above the 95th percentile. Cited only.

05 / What is checked

Verified, and not verified

Verified

  • The two tables are CDC’s files, unchanged (SHA-256 cbeea0e8d500… and 7f416a213157…, checked by the site’s tests).
  • The script reproduces all 24 values CDC prints in its five worked examples, recomputes 12702 printed percentiles and z-scores of the 2022 table within one unit of the last digit, and refuses to write any result if a worked example differs. (evidence)
  • Of the six fictional children, child E scores at the 99.84th percentile by the extended method, 99.44 by the LMS formula, whose z-score can never pass 3.110 for that row; child F, under 2, gets no percentile. (evidence)
  • On every pipeline, CI reruns the script with Python 3.12 and fails if any output differs from the files on this page. (evidence)

Not verified

  • Any real child, patient or programme. The children are fictional; nothing here is a screening, a clinical assessment, a diagnosis or medical advice, and BMI is a screening measure, not a diagnosis.
  • Weight-for-age, height-for-age, the WHO growth standards CDC recommends under age 2, and how height and weight are measured.
  • Ages between table rows: no interpolation, and the sigma regression CDC gives for ages finer than a month is not used.
  • Whether CDC’s categories suit any population or clinical setting, which is a question for a qualified reviewer.
  • Use by real learners: no learner has run these katas yet, and no real person’s measurements are used.

Professional review: None. No paediatrician, registered dietitian or epidemiologist has reviewed this pathway; any real child needs a qualified clinician, and any real programme the review its own governance requires.

Terms of the field

BMI-for-age percentile
Where a child’s body mass index sits among children of the same sex and age in CDC’s reference population; the 85th percentile means higher than 85 % of them.
LMS parameters
For each sex and month of age, the power (L), median (M) and coefficient of variation (S) from which CDC’s 2000 charts give a z-score: z = ((BMI / M)^L - 1) / (L S).
Extended BMI-for-age
CDC’s 2022 method above the 95th percentile: percentile = 90 + 10 Phi((BMI - P95) / sigma), which spreads out the highest values the LMS formula compresses.
z-score ceiling
Because L is negative in every row, the LMS z-score can never reach -1 / (L S), however high the BMI; for a 12-year-old boy that is 3.110.
Age at the half month
The tables list age as 48.5 months for 48.0 up to 49.0 completed months, so a child’s completed months plus 0.5 selects the row.
Severe obesity
CDC’s category for a BMI at or above 120 % of the 95th percentile for sex and age, a category of its own beside obesity.
Person-first language
Naming the person before the condition, such as a child with obesity, as CDC asks.

Procedures practised

  1. Inspect a growth reference table: its columns, row keys and age rule, before using it (HLT-001)
  2. Compute a BMI-for-age percentile and z-score with the LMS method and, above the 95th percentile, the extended method (HLT-002)
  3. Report BMI categories with CDC’s cut-offs, in person-first language, with the limits stated (HLT-003)
