Brand, website and mockups

  • Name and promise

    Choose a product name with evidence, check that it is free to use, write a one-sentence promise you can keep and test whether people remember and understand it.

  • Voice, colour, type and the brand sheet

    Define a tone of voice in three words, pick two colours and one font with contrast checked, write plain copy and put it all on one page that people and agents follow.

    Run basic accessibility checks, test with the keyboard and a screen reader, watch one person use your site without helping, and turn what you see into fixes and a decision log.

Frameworks

  • The frameworks map

    One picture of how UBF, STAR, ORBITAL, ANDROID Ops, DROID Ops, PHANTOMS and Launch Sequence fit together, from the whole industry down to one line of code.

  • The SuperNova path

    The eight stages an idea passes through from first dream to a product that has taken off, rewritten in plain words, with what each stage produces and what a developer does in it.

  • Frameworks cheat sheet

    Every framework and acronym in the track, spelled out with one line each, the question it answers and the artefact it produces. Keep it open while you work.

  • Directing agents across the frameworks

    How a Product Development Manager hands the routine work at every scale to AI agents, keeps the decisions, and verifies what comes back, using droid cards and a shared context file.

  • UBF: the business galaxy

    The Universal Business Framework pictures an industry as a galaxy. Learn every term, and use the map to choose integrations, spot platform risk and read big events.

  • NAICS, SIC and your galaxy

    How to use official industry codes (NAICS, SIC, and the international ISIC and NACE) to define the boundary of a galaxy, find real data about it and avoid arguing about words.

  • UBF, consulting levels and decisions

    Five altitudes of advice, from the whole industry down to hands-on building, and a simple portfolio method that uses UBF to decide what to start, grow, watch or stop across many projects.

  • STAR: describe a project

    Space, Track, Assist and Reach: four questions that let anyone see where a project sits, where it is going, how others plug into it and what it will achieve.

  • STAR in daily work

    Use SMART and STAR together for daily stand-ups and task reports, and see how STAR feeds the roadmap, the integrations register, the galaxy page and the ORBITAL indicators.

  • SHIFT: the focus shift

    A focus shift is a protected four-hour block used to change a project's course. Learn when to call one, how to plan it with the seven ANDROID activities and how to judge whether the plan is good.

  • ORBITAL: objective, resources and budget

    The first three letters of ORBITAL. Write one measurable objective, list what you need to reach it, and plan the money in time order, with a clear split between what you decide and what an agent drafts.

  • ORBITAL: indicators, KPIs and the SDGs

    Choose the numbers that tell you whether a product works. Learn what a KPI is, how to pick a few good ones, how to link a plan to the UN Sustainable Development Goals without overclaiming, and how to check an agent's data work.

  • ORBITAL: transport for software

    Transport is how goods, data, energy and value move from A to B. For a software product that means interfaces, data flows, files, releases and, often, physical things too. Learn to map them, choose a mode and judge what an agent proposes.

  • ORBITAL: activities and logistics

    The last two letters of ORBITAL. Break the plan into tasks and milestones in time order, then add the glue that keeps it running: coordination, communication, risks and a plan for learning. Decide, delegate and verify with agents.

  • ORBITAL: TrailMug in full

    One complete ORBITAL plan for the fictional TrailMug pilot, all seven letters on a page you can copy, plus a review method that tests whether the letters agree with each other.

  • From ORBITAL to milestones and issues

    Turn a finished ORBITAL plan into GitLab milestones, issues and labels that people and agents can work from. The plan stays yours, and the issues carry its limits, its proofs and its owners.

  • Why a new operating model

    Waterfall, Agile, DevOps and MLOps each solved one problem. ANDROID Ops joins them into one loop that a Product Development Manager can run with people and AI agents.

  • ANDROID focus areas 1 to 4: Advance, Network, Design, Research

    The first four ANDROID Ops focus areas in depth: what each one is for, the activities inside it, who does what, what the Product Development Manager does, and what agents can take.

  • ANDROID focus areas 5 to 7: Optimize, Implement, Deliver

    The last three ANDROID Ops focus areas in depth: measure and improve, build and test with agents under your direction, and release and support with proof.

  • DROID Ops for programmes, content and training

    The lighter five-step loop (Design, Research, Optimize, Integrate, Deliver) for training, content and programme work, using the Dojo itself as the example.

  • Running ANDROID Ops in GitLab

    Set up labels, milestones, issues, boards, pipelines and change records so the seven focus areas run in git, with people and AI agents sharing one visible plan.

Guides

Product discovery

  • Frame the problem and the idea

    Turn a loose idea into a short written brief with a user, a problem, a measure and a date, and list the assumptions that must be true before you spend months building.

  • Talk to customers

    Recruit the right people, run five short interviews that ask about the past, and capture evidence you can trust without leaking anyone's private details.

  • Map the whole product

    Draw your product on one page: every part, every rented system, every partner and every competitor or workaround, with a clear mark for what exists today and what does not.

  • Make sense of the evidence and decide

    Cluster what you learned, grade the strength of your evidence, test your riskiest assumption cheaply and write a decision record that says what you will build, what you will not and what would change your mind.

Roadmapping and epics

  • What an epic is, and what it is not

    Learn the four levels of planning, why an epic is not just a big issue, and three size tests that tell an epic from a milestone or an issue.

  • Write an epic yourself

    Write a complete epic by hand, line by line: the person, the problem, a measurable outcome, scope in and out, the parts touched, dependencies, risks and an owner.

  • Order the epics and defend the order

    Order your epics by value, risk and dependency, write one sentence of reasoning for each position, choose a prioritisation method on purpose and say what you chose not to do.

  • Your roadmap in git

    Run the whole roadmap on a free GitLab account with issues, milestones, labels and boards, keep a plain-text copy in the repo and know what the paid epics feature would add.

  • Agent-ready milestones

    Cut your first epic into milestones with acceptance checks a person can test, hand a milestone to an agent with a written brief, review the result and keep a decision log.

Security fundamentals

  • Threats and the OWASP Top 10

    Who attacks software and how, the families of weakness that cause most breaches, and the plain questions that turn them into security requirements and review checks.

  • Secrets and identity

    Where passwords, keys and tokens may live and where they may not, how they leak, how to find them, what to do in the first hour after a leak, and why narrow identities beat shared keys.

  • Supply chain and dependencies

    Almost all of your product is other people's code. How to judge a dependency, read a bill of materials, and understand what signatures and build provenance prove and do not prove.

Guides

Stakeholder communication

  • Map the stakeholders

    List everyone affected by your product, learn what each wants, fears and can decide, and turn the list into a table that tells you who to talk to, how often and in what form.

  • Write the one-pager and the weekly note

    Write a one-page brief that states the goal, people and risks, and a short weekly note that reports what shipped, what did not, what you learned and what you need, in words busy people read.

  • Present and defend the roadmap

    Prepare a ten-minute roadmap presentation that shows the order, the reasons, the risks and the not-doing list, then answer hard questions without bluffing and record what you change.

  • Say no, report risk and deliver bad news

    Handle requests that do not fit, turn risks into written statements with owners, deliver bad news early and plainly, and tell people honestly where AI agents were used.

  • H is for Hono

  • A is for AI: the replaceable adapter

Working with AI agents

  • How AI agents work

    A plain-words model of what a language model is, what turns it into an agent, and why it can sound sure while being wrong.

  • Prompting that works

    Write clear tasks for an AI model: goal, context, constraints, examples and output format, and a loop for improving a prompt one change at a time.

  • Context and instructions

    Decide what an agent can see, keep that context small and relevant, and write a project instruction file that every session starts from.

  • Specs and acceptance criteria

    Turn a decision into a specification an agent can build and you can verify: problem, scope, acceptance criteria, limits and examples.

  • Agentic coding tools in practice

    Run a coding agent safely on real work: one issue, one branch, plan first, small steps, tests as the judge, and permissions you control.

  • Orchestrating multiple agents

    Run several agents at once on separate issues without losing control: split work, isolate branches, define roles, sequence reviews and keep one human accountable.

  • Reviewing and verifying AI output

    A review method for AI-written code, text and data: how to read a diff, spot plausible nonsense and decide what to accept, fix or reject.

  • Evals and testing AI work

    Measure an AI workflow instead of guessing: build a small test set, score the results, compare changes and catch regressions.

  • Safety, security and trust

    Keep secrets and systems safe when agents act: least privilege, prompt injection, safe sandboxes, human approval and what never goes into a chat.

  • Disclosure and ownership

    Tell people honestly when and how you used AI, handle licences and credit, and keep the epics, roadmap and decisions in human hands.
